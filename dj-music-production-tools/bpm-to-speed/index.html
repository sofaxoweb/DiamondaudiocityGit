Last updated: October 23, 2026 · Written by the Pitch Detectors Editorial Team

A BPM to speed calculator converts a tempo change into a playback speed multiplier and a one-line verdict. Moving a 100 BPM track to 120 BPM needs 1.20× speed — the calculator reports "20% faster." From that single multiplier, this page derives the exact pitch percentage, the semitone shift, and the new duration. DJs, producers, and video editors use these ratios to match tempos, shift keys, and retime media precisely.

Key Takeaways

  • Speed multiplier = target BPM ÷ original BPM — 120 ÷ 100 = 1.20×.
  • Pitch % = (target − original) ÷ original × 100 — the same change reads as +20%.
  • +1 semitone = ×1.05946 speed (+5.946%) — the ratio behind every key change.
  • Duration runs inverse: new length = old length ÷ multiplier.
  • Key lock holds pitch across tempo changes but smears transients past roughly ±10%.

What Is BPM to Speed Conversion?

Definition: BPM to speed conversion expresses a tempo change as a playback speed multiplier. Raising a track from 100 to 120 BPM means playing it 1.20× faster — a +20% pitch increase. The multiplier, the percentage, and the semitone shift are three views of one identical change.

Speed and pitch move together in traditional playback: spin a record faster and it plays higher as well as quicker. Digital key lock (Master Tempo) separates the two, but the underlying ratio never changes. Beatmatching on CDJs, warping a sample in a DAW, and speeding up a podcast apply the same math.

BPM to Speed Formulas and Worked Example

Three formulas cover every conversion:

  • Speed multiplier = Target BPM ÷ Original BPM
  • Pitch change % = (Target − Original) ÷ Original × 100
  • Semitones = 12 × log₂(Speed multiplier) — cents = semitones × 100

Worked example: pitching a 100 BPM track up to 120 BPM:

  1. Multiplier: 120 ÷ 100 = 1.20×
  2. Pitch change: (120 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100 = +20%
  3. Semitones: 12 × log₂(1.20) = 12 × 0.263 = +3.16 semitones (about a minor third)
  4. Duration effect: a 4-minute track now runs 4 ÷ 1.20 = 3 minutes 20 seconds

Second example — the everyday DJ pairing, 120 → 128 BPM: multiplier 128 ÷ 120 = 1.0667×; pitch change +6.67%; semitones 12 × log₂(1.0667) = +1.12 semitones. The calculator displays this pair as 1.07× with a "7% faster" verdict — correct to its two-decimal display, but the exact figures above are the ones to use when judging whether the key still fits the next track.

The Three Numbers, Disentangled

Multiplier, speed %, and pitch % describe the identical change from different starting points. Speed % = multiplier × 100, and pitch % = speed % − 100. So 1.20× = 120% speed = +20% pitch. Confusion usually starts at the hardware: Pioneer-style faders display pitch % (±8%), while DAW warp dialogs and video players display speed multiplier or % (1.00×). Convert between the two by adding or subtracting 100.

The semitone figure is the musician's view of the same multiplier: it shows how far the key moves, which decides harmonic mixing. A +20% pitch shift is +3.16 semitones — enough to clash with the outgoing track unless key lock is engaged or the keys are compatible.

Precision note: the calculator's verdict rounds twice — the multiplier to two decimals, then the percentage to a whole number, taken from the rounded multiplier. So 128→130 BPM reads "2% faster" although the exact change is +1.56%, or +0.27 semitones. For key-critical work, compute from the unrounded ratio (target ÷ original) with the formulas above instead of the displayed verdict.

Key Lock On or Off? The Quality Tradeoff

Key lock (Master Tempo on Pioneer, Keylock on Denon) time-stretches audio to change tempo while holding pitch constant. It makes harmonic mixing possible across wide BPM gaps. The price is audio quality: time-stretch smears transients, and the artifacts turn audible past roughly ±10% — first on vocals, acoustic drums, and heavily compressed audio.

Practical policy: key lock ON for harmonic blends wider than ±6%, where a key clash is the bigger danger; key lock OFF for small adjustments (±3% or less), scratching, and turntablism, where the natural pitch nudge sounds better and the algorithm would only add smear. A phasey or "underwater" sound with key lock on is the algorithm itself — narrow the gap or switch it off.

Speed Math for Video Editors

The same ratios retime video. Converting 24 fps film to 25 fps PAL is a 25÷24 = 1.0417× speed-up (+4.17% pitch — the reason PAL audio sounds slightly sharp). Slow motion at 50% speed doubles duration and drops pitch one octave. Learning playback at 0.75× stretches a 4-minute lesson to 5:20 and drops pitch about 5 semitones — fine for watching, useless for playing along without pitch-preserving slowdown.

Limitations

  • Two-decimal display. The multiplier is rounded to two decimals and the verdict percentage is calculated from that rounded value, so small changes display coarsely: 128→130 BPM shows "2% faster" for an exact change of +1.56%.
  • Very small changes read as "No change." When the ratio rounds to 1.00×, the verdict says "No change — same speed as the original BPM" even if the tempos differ slightly — entering 120 → 120.5 BPM does this (verified against the tool). Use exact ratios from the formulas for sub-1% work.
  • Linear resampling assumed. The math describes simple speed change; key-lock time-stretch follows the same ratios and adds algorithm artifacts the numbers cannot show.
  • Extreme shifts degrade audio. Past about ±25%, even strong time-stretch sounds unnatural — plan transitions instead of forcing the gap.
  • Equal-temperament math. Real instruments drift from perfect 12-TET; a calculated +3.00 semitones can beat slightly against a live-recorded track.
  • No swing or groove modeling. Ratios are exact; feel is not — a mathematically perfect match can still sound wrong on swung material.
