BPM Ranges by Genre: Typical Tempo Chart

Last updated: October 18, 2026 · Written by the Pitch Detectors Editorial Team

Dance music genres cluster around signature tempos: house sits near 120–130 BPM, techno at 125–145, dubstep at 138–145, and drum and bass at 170–176. These BPM ranges are conventions, not rules — they grew out of dance floors, equipment limits, and production habits. This guide charts typical tempos across 25+ genres, explains why ranges overlap and drift, and shows how DJs use them to plan mixes, energy, and cross-genre transitions.

Key Takeaways

  • Typical ranges: house 115–130, techno 125–145, trance 128–140, dubstep 138–145, drum and bass 170–176, hip-hop 80–100, reggaeton 88–98, dembow 95–110 BPM.
  • BPM ranges are guidelines. Catalog data shows real songs routinely landing outside the published ranges.
  • Half-time drum patterns make 140 BPM dubstep feel like 70 BPM — and DJ software often reports the half-time number by mistake.
  • Overlap is normal: house and trance both live near 128–130 BPM. BPM alone never identifies a genre.
  • DJs build sets as BPM arcs (warm-up → peak → close) and use the pitch fader — ±8% is about ±10 BPM at 128 — to bridge adjacent tempos.
  • New to the term itself? Start with what BPM means in music.

Why Genres Cluster at Certain Tempos

Genre tempos converge for three practical reasons: bodies, equipment, and momentum. BPM ranges are not random — each reason locks a genre into its lane.

Bodies come first. Dancing has a comfortable cadence, and music follows it. Music-cognition research going back to Moelants (2002) puts preferred tapping tempo around 120–130 BPM. That helps explain why so many dance genres independently converged on the 120s: it is fast enough to energize a room and sustainable enough to dance to for hours. Genres built for listening rather than dancing (hip-hop, R&B, reggaeton) settled lower, where lyrics and groove have room to breathe.

Equipment set the lanes. Vinyl turntables like the Technics SL-1200 offered a ±8% pitch range, so tracks had to sit within about ±10 BPM of each other to mix. Producers aiming for club play naturally wrote inside the mixable window of neighboring genres, and the whole ladder in the chart above reflects that constraint.

Momentum keeps them there. Once a genre's classics establish a tempo, new producers copy reference tracks, sample packs ship at that tempo, and DJ pools sort by it. The range becomes self-reinforcing — until a new scene deliberately breaks it, the way dubstep slowed UK garage's 130s down to a half-time 140.

Half-Time Feels: Why 140 Sounds Like 70

Half-time drums make 140 BPM music feel like 70 BPM. Dubstep is produced at 138–145 BPM, yet dancers and listeners describe it as slow. The trick is in the drums: the snare lands every two beats instead of every beat, so your body locks onto a pulse at half the notated tempo.

The math is simple. At 140 BPM, each quarter-note beat lasts 60 ÷ 140 = 0.43 seconds. A half-time snare pattern puts the felt pulse at 70 BPM, where each beat lasts 60 ÷ 70 = 0.86 seconds — twice as long. Your ears hear the 140 hi-hats; your body follows the 70 snare.

DJ software misreads these genres for the same reason. Detection algorithms often lock onto the half-time drum pattern and report 70 BPM for a 140 BPM dubstep track (or 87 for a 174 drum and bass track counted at half). When beatmatching, use the notated tempo — the fader math only works against the grid your software is actually playing. Three patterns stand out. First, the centers agree: house clusters near 124–128 everywhere, dubstep at 140 everywhere. Second, the edges are editorial — each publisher draws the fence where its audience's library ends. Third, the outliers reveal method: SirenMix lists dubstep as "70 or 140" because it writes for DJs fixing beatgrids, not producers picking project tempos.

The lesson: read the center of any BPM chart as convention and the edges as one author's judgment. When the number matters — a mix, a production — check the actual track with a tap tempo tool instead of trusting any chart.

How DJs Use BPM Ranges in a Set

DJs use genre tempos as an energy arc, not a fixed speed. The standard plan: open around 115–122 BPM while the floor fills, climb to a 126–132 peak, then ease back toward 118–124 for the close. BPM ranges tell the DJ which records can carry each phase of that arc.

The pitch fader bridges adjacent genres. A ±8% fader at 128 BPM covers 128 × 0.92 = 117.8 up to 128 × 1.08 = 138.2 — about ±10 BPM. The table shows what that reach buys at three deck tempos:

Deck tempo±8% spanGenres inside the span
120 BPM110.4–129.6Nu disco, deep house, house, tech house
128 BPM117.8–138.2Tech house, techno, trance, UK garage
140 BPM128.8–151.2Trance, psytrance, dubstep, trap, hardstyle

For bigger jumps, DJs use transition records at middle tempos, mix during breakdowns where the beat drops out, or switch the deck to a wide tempo range (±16% or more) with key lock engaged. Our BPM to speed converter runs the fader math for any two tempos.

Ranges warn against lazy mixing. Two genres can share a BPM and still clash — 128 BPM big-room house and 128 BPM trance have different drum weights, bass designs, and breakdown structures, so the transition needs phrasing and EQ work, not just matched numbers. Subgenre Zoom: Where the Families Split

Inside each genre family, subgenres carve out narrower BPM ranges. Knowing them helps producers pick project tempos and helps DJs sort crates:

  • House family: nu disco (110–122) → deep house (115–125) → afro house (118–125) → tech house (122–130) → big room (126–130). Tempo rises with intensity.
  • Techno family: dub techno (118–128) → melodic techno (122–130) → peak-time techno (132–145) → hard techno (145–160). The harder the kick, the faster the grid.
  • Bass family: dubstep (138–145, half-time feel) → trap (140–150, half-time feel) → hardstyle (145–155) → footwork (150–165) → hardcore (160–200). Speed and aggression scale together here.
  • Breakbeat family: UK garage (130–135) → jungle (155–170) → drum and bass (170–176). Broken rhythms run faster than four-on-the-floor at the same energy level — the syncopation needs the extra speed to hit.

Common Mistakes With Genre BPM Ranges

Most BPM-range mistakes come from treating conventions as rules. Five errors show up constantly:

  • Treating ranges as rules. A 131 BPM track in a "128 genre" is not wrong — charts describe centers, not fences. Judge the track, not the number.
  • Trusting auto-detected BPM in half-time genres. Software reads 70 for 140 BPM dubstep and trap constantly. If a reading looks halved, double it before beatmatching.
  • Assuming overlap means interchangeability. 128 BPM house and 128 BPM trance mix at the same speed but carry different energy, drums, and breakdowns. Match the vibe, not just the number.
  • Pitching too far without key lock. Beyond about ±4%, vocals and melodic elements start sounding noticeably off. Big tempo jumps need key lock or a planned transition.
  • Playing a whole set at one BPM. A flat 128 all night fatigues the floor. The arc — up and back down — is what makes peak moments feel like peaks.

At the same BPM, genres differ in drum patterns, sound design, and history — not speed. If BPM ranges overlap so much, what actually separates genres at the same tempo? Everything except speed:
