    What Is Mix Compatibility?

    Mix compatibility is a measure of whether two tracks can be blended smoothly in a DJ set. It judges two things: do the musical keys harmonize (so melodies and basslines don't clash), and are the tempos close enough to beatmatch without audible pitch damage? When both pass, a transition sounds like one continuous piece of music.

    DJs call the key side harmonic mixing — choosing the next track by musical key instead of feel alone. The tempo side is beatmatching: aligning beats per minute so both tracks run in sync. Most train-wreck transitions fail on one of these two axes, which is why this checker tests both at once. If you don't know a track's key yet, run it through our Song Key Finder first.

    The two scores are added together for the overall verdict. The wheel math behind the key score comes from the circle of fifths: neighboring Camelot numbers sit a fifth apart and share nearly all their scale notes, which is why ±1 mixes sound smooth while distant jumps clash. The BPM bands come from pitch-fader math: 3 BPM at 128 is 2.3%, 6 BPM is 4.7%, 10 BPM is 7.8% — each band sits at a meaningfully different point on a ±8% fader.

    Compatibility Score Formula and Worked Example

    Total score = Key score (−1 to +3) + BPM score (−1 to +3)

    Worked example: Track A is 128 BPM in 8A; Track B is 126 BPM in 9A.

    1. Key score: 8A → 9A is +1 step, same letter = Adjacent Key = +2.
    2. BPM score: |128 − 126| = 2 BPM difference = Easy Mix = +2.
    3. Total: 2 + 2 = 4 → Good Match. The key rises a fifth while the tempo nudge stays inside the easy zone.

    Contrast: 8A at 128 BPM against 3B at 128 BPM scores key −1 (Key Clash) and BPM +3 (Exact Match) for a total of 2 — only a Possible Mix. Tempo alone never guarantees a good blend.

    Tier boundaries, checked: 8A/128 vs 8A/128 = 3 + 3 = 6 (Excellent); 8A/128 vs 10A/135 = 0 + 0 = 0 (Not Recommended); 8A/128 vs 9B/131 = 1 + 2 = 3 (Good, at the floor of the tier). The rubric has no hidden weights — what you see is what the widget computes.

    Pro Mixing Techniques

    Energy boost mixing: raising intensity a perfect fifth

    Moving +1 Camelot number (e.g. 8A → 9A) shifts the music up a perfect fifth — 7 semitones. Our ears hear a fifth as a lift: the same melody feels brighter and more urgent. DJs use this "energy boost" to escalate a set before a peak-time track; moving −1 does the reverse and cools the room. One caveat from Mixed In Key itself: the key change alone doesn't guarantee a rise — loudness, groove, and where you enter the track decide whether the room actually lifts. Try the interval first with our Key Transposition tool.

    Why ±3 BPM is the "easy" line

    The widget calls a 1–3 BPM gap an Easy Mix because of pitch-fader math. A standard DJ pitch fader spans ±8% (Pioneer CDJ). At 128 BPM, 3 BPM is only a 2.3% shift — comfortably inside the fader, with no audible strain. A 10+ BPM gap needs ~8% or more, which sits at the fader's edge and is where most DJs engage key lock or choose another track.

    Key lock: when it saves a mix and when it wrecks it

    Key lock (Master Tempo) holds a track's musical pitch steady while you change its tempo, using time-stretching. It saves mixes with 6–10 BPM gaps — but past roughly ±6% shift, time-stretching smears transients and thins vocals, an artifact dancers can hear. Rule of thumb: inside ±3 BPM, leave key lock off; from ±4 to ±8 BPM, switch it on; past that, pick a closer track.

    Half-time and double-time transitions

    Tracks at a 0.5× or 2× tempo ratio — say 70 BPM against 140 BPM — share the same beat grid: every downbeat of the slower track lands on the faster one's. That makes them mixable despite a huge BPM number gap. Hip-hop DJs ride 70↔140 transitions constantly; the trick is matching phrases, not just beats, so the switch lands on a natural break.

    Honest note: this calculator's rubric has no ratio exception, so it scores these pairs Difficult. That warning is correct for naive blends — and wrong when your phrases align. Override the score by ear here.

    Camelot vs Open Key Notation

    Camelot (1A–12B, from Mixed In Key) and Open Key (1m–12d, used by Traktor and Lexicon) describe the same 24 keys with different labels. "m" means minor (the Camelot A side), "d" means major (the B side, from German dur). Use whichever your DJ software displays — the mixing rules are identical.

    CamelotOpen KeyMusical key
    8A1mA minor
    8B1dC major
    9A2mE minor
    9B2dG major
    11A4mF♯ minor
    11B4dA major

    Converting is mechanical: subtract 7 from the Camelot number (wrapping around 1–12), then swap A→m and B→d. 8A becomes 1m, 9B becomes 2d, 11A becomes 4m. Both wheels ascend by fifths in the same direction — Open Key simply starts its count at C major / A minor (1d / 1m). Rekordbox and Serato show Camelot-style codes; Traktor shows Open Key — check which one your library uses before a gig.

    Limitations

    • Key detection isn't perfect. Software key analysis mislabels a real share of tracks — one survey of tools puts errors anywhere from 14% to 59% depending on the tool (digitaldjpool). If a "Perfect Match" sounds wrong, trust your ears and re-analyze.
    • Energy and phrasing aren't scored. Two compatible tracks still clash when a vocal sits on the other's drop — phrase matching matters as much as key.
    • No half-time/double-time exception. The rubric scores 70-vs-140 BPM pairs Difficult even when the beat grids align; override it when phrases match.
    • Genre conventions differ. Hip-hop and drum & bass DJs routinely break harmonic rules with cuts and doubles; the rubric fits smooth house/techno-style blending best.
    • Live key changes fool the math. Tracks that modulate mid-song (common in pop) get one label but contain two keys.

    This tool is for informational and practice purposes. Key and tempo analysis is an estimate — always confirm by ear before a live set.
