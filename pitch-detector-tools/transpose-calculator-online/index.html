Transpose Calculator: Shift Notes, Chords & Keys

Last updated: October 12, 2026 · Written by the Pitch Detectors Editorial Team

A transpose calculator shifts notes, chords, or a full song up or down by any number of semitones while keeping every interval exactly the same. Type your notes, set the shift, and get the transposed result instantly — no manual counting around the chromatic scale.

Key Takeaways

  • Transposition moves every note by the same interval, so a melody keeps its character and harmony — it only sounds higher or lower.
  • One semitone = one half step = one guitar fret. Twelve semitones = one octave.
  • A capo on fret n raises the sounding pitch n semitones; transposing a chart up while moving the capo down by the same frets keeps the original key.
  • B♭ trumpet sounds a major 2nd (2 semitones) below its written pitch; E♭ alto sax sounds a major 6th (9 semitones) below.
  • This calculator outputs sharp spellings (F♯, C♯); flats like G♭ are the same pitch — respell to match your key signature.

What Is Musical Transposition?

Musical transposition is the process of shifting a melody, chord progression, or complete piece up or down in pitch by a fixed interval while preserving all internal interval relationships. Because every note moves the same distance, the music keeps its harmonic character — it simply sounds in a higher or lower key. Singers, guitarists, and arrangers transpose daily: to fit a vocal range, to use a capo, or to rewrite parts for transposing instruments.

A singer may need a song two semitones lower to sit in a comfortable range. A guitarist clamps a capo on the 2nd fret to play easy open shapes in a higher key. An arranger rewrites a concert-pitch piano part for B♭ trumpet, shifting every note up a major 2nd so it sounds at the right pitch.

How This Transpose Calculator Works

This calculator treats the twelve chromatic pitches as numbered positions 0–11 (C = 0 through B = 11) and adds your semitone shift with wraparound arithmetic. Flat spellings are accepted — typing D♭ or B♭ normalizes them to sharp equivalents (C♯, A♯) before transposing. Octave crossings adjust the octave number automatically.

Methodology: new index = (note index + semitones + octave shift × 12) mod 12. We verified the widget against standard transposition references with five sample inputs: C, F, and G shifted up 4 semitones produced E, A, and B, and E4 shifted down 4 semitones produced C4 — matching manual chromatic-scale counting exactly.

Transposition Formula and Worked Example

Transposing is addition on the chromatic scale. Count the starting note as position 0, then move up or down the requested number of half steps:

Transposed note = start note + semitones (mod 12)

Worked example: transpose the I–IV–V progression in C major (C – F – G) up 4 semitones:

  1. C + 4: C→C♯(1)→D(2)→D♯(3)→E(4)
  2. F + 4: F→F♯(1)→G(2)→G♯(3)→A(4)
  3. G + 4: G→G♯(1)→A(2)→A♯(3)→B(4)
  4. Result: E – A – B, which is the I–IV–V progression in E major. Same shape, new key.

How to Use the Transpose Calculator

  1. Enter notes/chords: type note names separated by commas (e.g. C, E, G). Use plain note names — chord extensions are read as octave numbers (see Limitations).
  2. Set semitones: enter a positive number to transpose up or a negative number to transpose down (e.g. -2).
  3. Octave shift (optional): move everything a full octave or more with the dropdown, independent of the semitone shift.
  4. Press Transpose (or hit Enter) to see the result; Copy Result pastes it into your chord chart or notation software.
  5. Keyboard shortcut: with the notes field unfocused, press ↑ / ↓ to nudge the transposition up or down one semitone at a time.

Example: you know your G-shape fingerings but the singer wants the song in A major — clamp the capo on fret 2 (+2 semitones: G→G♯→A) and play your familiar shapes, which now sound in A. To reach B from G shapes, use fret 4 (+4 semitones: G→G♯→A→A♯→B). Enter G, C, D with +4 semitones in the calculator above to confirm: B, E, F♯.

The reverse direction confuses most beginners. A capo raises the sounding pitch; this calculator changes the written notes. To keep a song in its original key after transposing the chart up, move the capo down by the same number of frets — transpose +2 (C→D on paper) and slide the capo from fret 3 to fret 1, and the audience still hears the original key. To drop the capo entirely, transpose the chart up by the capo's fret number: a capo-3 chart transposed +3 plays with no capo at the same pitch.

Enharmonic Spelling: F♯ or G♭?

After transposing, you may get a sharp spelling where a flat reads better — or the reverse. F♯ and G♭ are the exact same pitch (enharmonic equivalents); the right spelling is the one that fits your key signature.

  • Sharp keys (G, D, A, E major): prefer sharps — write F♯, C♯, G♯.
  • Flat keys (F, B♭, E♭, A♭ major): prefer flats — write G♭, D♭, B♭.
  • Rule of thumb: each letter name should appear once per scale. If your transposed scale already has a G, the altered note is better spelled as F♯ than G♭.

This calculator always outputs sharp spellings, so in flat keys do a quick find-and-replace: C♯→D♭, F♯→G♭, G♯→A♭, A♯→B♭, D♯→E♭.

Transposing for a Vocalist: A 4-Step Workflow

Moving a song into a singer's comfortable key is the most common real-world transposition. Follow these steps:

  1. Find the original key. Check the key signature or the final chord of the song (e.g. original key: E major).
  2. Find the singer's key. Have them hum the chorus; identify the key where the highest note sits comfortably (e.g. they top out happily in C major).
  3. Count the semitones. E down to C is 4 semitones (E→E♭ = 1, D = 2, D♭ = 3, C = 4), so transpose by −4.
  4. Transpose and sanity-check the range. Run the chord progression through the calculator at −4, then confirm the new highest and lowest melody notes sit inside the singer's range — transposing down drags the low notes down too.

The Nashville Number Shortcut

The Nashville Number System writes chords as scale degrees (1, 4, 5) instead of letter names, which makes a progression key-independent. "1 – 5 – 6 – 4" is C–G–Am–F in C major and G–D–Em–C in G major — no recalculation needed.

Use it with this calculator in two passes: convert the progression to numbers in the original key once, and you never transpose again — just read the numbers in any new key. Session players in country and pop studios use it daily because a bandleader can call "take it up a whole step" mid-rehearsal and nobody rewrites a chart. Limitations

  • Flat inputs are normalized to sharps. Typing B♭ or E♭ works — flats are converted to their sharp equivalents (A♯, D♯) before transposing. Output always uses sharp spellings.
  • Chord extensions are read as octaves. Entering G7 is interpreted as the note G in octave 7, not a G dominant-7th chord. Transpose the root (G) and re-add the extension (7) yourself.
  • Output uses sharp spellings only. See the enharmonic section above for converting to flats in flat keys.
  • Key signatures are not rewritten. The calculator moves pitches; you still need to update the key signature on your sheet music.
  • Twelve-tone equal temperament assumed. Historical temperaments and microtonal music divide the octave differently.
  • Capo math is sounding pitch. The tool shifts written notes; it does not track which fret your hand plays. Use the reverse-arithmetic rule in the capo section to convert between the two.

Frequently Asked Questions

Musical transposition shifts a melody, chord progression, or entire piece up or down by a fixed interval while preserving all internal interval relationships. The song keeps its harmonic character — it sounds in a higher or lower key. Singers use it to fit their range; arrangers use it to rewrite parts for transposing instruments.

One semitone equals one half step: a single piano key or guitar fret. A whole step is 2 semitones, and an octave is 12. The common transpositions are minor 3rd = 3, major 3rd = 4, perfect 4th = 5, and perfect 5th = 7 semitones.

Each capo fret raises the sounding pitch one semitone. Enter your chord roots with a positive shift equal to the fret number to find the sounding chords — G shapes with capo 2 (+2) sound in A. Reverse it: enter the target key's chords with a negative shift to find which shapes to finger under the capo.

B♭ trumpet sounds a major 2nd (2 semitones) below its written pitch, so write the part 2 semitones above concert pitch. Enter the concert-pitch notes with +2 semitones. Use +9 for E♭ alto sax and +7 for F horn.

Concert pitch is the actual sounding pitch — what a piano plays and the audience hears. Written pitch is what appears on a transposing instrument's sheet music. They match for piano, flute, and violin; for B♭ trumpet, written C sounds as concert B♭.

Write the spelling that fits the key signature — both are the same pitch. Sharp keys (G, D, A major) take sharps; flat keys (F, B♭, E♭ major) take flats. Keep one letter name per scale degree so no letter repeats.

Find the original key and the singer's comfortable key, count the semitones between them, and transpose the whole progression by that amount. Check the new lowest notes too — lowering a key can drag verses below the singer's usable range.

The interval structure stays identical, so the harmonic character does not change. Shifts of 1–2 semitones are barely noticeable; larger shifts change brightness and energy because the pitch moves into a different part of the voice's and instruments' ranges.

The Nashville Number System writes chords as scale degrees (1, 4, 5, 6m) instead of letter names, making a progression playable in every key. It is the standard shorthand in country and pop session work, where leaders call key changes mid-rehearsal.

The engine outputs sharp spellings (F♯, C♯) by design. They are enharmonically identical to flats (G♭, D♭) — the same piano key — so respell to flats in flat key signatures. Flat inputs like B♭ or E♭ are accepted and transposed normally; only the output is sharp-only.
