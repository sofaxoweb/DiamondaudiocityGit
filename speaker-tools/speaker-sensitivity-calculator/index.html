What is speaker sensitivity?

Speaker sensitivity is the loudness a speaker produces, in decibels, when fed a standard signal and measured from 1 meter away. It is written as dB SPL at 1W/1m, or sometimes dB at 2.83V/1m. Typical home speakers land between 85 and 92 dB; horn-loaded designs reach 95 dB and up, while some compact sealed speakers sit near 80–84 dB.

Think of sensitivity as the speaker's efficiency at turning electricity into sound. A speaker rated 91 dB needs half the amplifier power of an 88 dB speaker to play equally loud. That 3 dB difference is the single most useful number when matching speakers to an amplifier — it decides whether a 30-watt amp is plenty or hopelessly underpowered.

How is sensitivity measured?

Manufacturers measure sensitivity in an anechoic chamber — a room with no reflections — with 1 watt into 8 ohms of pink noise or a swept tone, averaged across the midband, microphone at exactly 1 meter. Some brands instead quote 2.83 V / 1 m, which equals 1 watt into 8 ohms — identical. Into 4 ohms, though, 2.83 V delivers 2 watts, which reads 3 dB louder.

The anechoic part matters. Your living room is not anechoic: walls, floor, and ceiling reflect sound back at you, adding roughly 3–6 dB of room gain in a typical furnished room. A speaker that measures 88 dB in the lab often delivers closer to 91–94 dB where you sit — the calculator ignores room gain on purpose, giving you a conservative worst-case number.

How this calculator works

The widget combines three physics facts into one estimate. Every doubling of amplifier power adds 3 dB: the 10 × log₁₀(power) term. Sound pressure falls with distance following the inverse square law, so every doubling of distance removes 6 dB: the 20 × log₁₀(distance) term. The speaker's sensitivity is the starting point — what it does with the first watt at one meter.

It then classifies the result two ways. The efficiency class rates the speaker itself: 95 dB or higher is High, 88–94 dB is Moderate, and below 88 dB is Low. The amplifier stress warning fires when you pair a low-sensitivity speaker (under 86 dB) with more than 200 watts — a combination that runs amps hot and flirts with clipping. The formula (used by the calculator)

SPL = sensitivity + 10 × log₁₀(power) − 20 × log₁₀(distance)

The result is rounded to the nearest whole decibel. The impedance selector (2/4/8 Ω) does not change the SPL number — see the FAQ below for why.

Worked example

Take a typical bookshelf speaker rated 88 dB, driven by a 100-watt amp, heard from 3 meters (about 10 feet):

  • Power gain: 10 × log₁₀(100) = +20 dB
  • Distance loss: 20 × log₁₀(3) ≈ −9.5 dB
  • SPL = 88 + 20 − 9.5 = 98.5 → 98 dB

That is roughly the level of a passing motorcycle — plenty for music, and exactly what the widget shows for these inputs.

How many watts do you actually need?

Work backwards from a target. Comfortable home listening wants peaks around 95–100 dB at the seat; home theater reference peaks hit about 105 dB. Rearranged: required watts = 10^((target − sensitivity + 20 × log₁₀(distance)) / 10).

Example: 90 dB speakers, 2.5-meter seat, 100 dB target → 10^((100 − 90 + 7.96) / 10) ≈ 62 watts. A 75–100 W amp gives comfortable headroom. Run the same math with 84 dB speakers and you need nearly 250 watts — that is the price of low sensitivity, paid in amplifier.

The 2.83V trap: why 4-ohm ratings read 3 dB high

2.83 volts into 8 Ω is exactly 1 watt — but into 4 Ω it is 2 watts, which plays 3 dB louder. The math: 2.83² / 8 = 1.0 W; 2.83² / 4 = 2.0 W; 10 × log₁₀(2) = 3.01 dB. A 4-ohm speaker rated "90 dB at 2.83V/1m" is really an 87 dB (1W/1m) speaker wearing a flattering spec.

Always check which standard the spec uses before comparing brands. Subtract 3 dB from 4-ohm 2.83V ratings when comparing against 8-ohm 1W ratings. Some calculators take 2.83V/1m as their input — convenient, but it bakes the inflation into every 4-ohm result.

Measured vs advertised: why specs lie

Independent measurements routinely come in 1–3 dB below the advertised sensitivity. Audio Science Review's measurement program has documented multiple mainstream speakers testing lower than their spec sheets claim — different test signals, smoothed curves, and generous rounding all flatter the number.

Treat the spec as a best case. When the calculator's prediction feels optimistic for your room, knock 2 dB off the sensitivity input and re-run — that is closer to what independent labs measure. Sensitivity still ranks speakers correctly; it is the absolute number you should distrust, not the ordering.

Matching sensitivity to your amplifier type

Pair the speaker's sensitivity to what your amplifier can actually deliver. Low-power amplifiers need efficient speakers; high-power amplifiers can brute-force inefficient ones:

High-power, 200 W+AnyBrute force works, but watch the stress warning above 200 W with sub-86 dB speakers

Common mistakes

  • Using peak watts: "200W peak" often means 100W RMS. Entering peak power inflates your SPL estimate.
  • Ignoring distance: moving from 2 m to 4 m costs 6 dB — equivalent to quadrupling your amp. Measure your actual seat.
  • Chasing watts instead of sensitivity: upgrading from an 86 dB to a 92 dB speaker buys 6 dB — the same as quadrupling amplifier power, usually for less money.
  • Forgetting headroom: music peaks run 10–20 dB above the average. An amp sized exactly for your target SPL clips on loud passages; leave 3–6 dB of headroom.
