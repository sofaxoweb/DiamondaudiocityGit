Key Takeaways

  • A Zobel is a resistor + capacitor in series, wired in parallel with the driver — not in line with it.
  • Rz = Re (voice-coil DC resistance, ≈ nominal impedance); Cz = 1 ÷ (2π × R × f) — or equivalently Cz = Le ÷ Re².
  • A Zobel fixes inductive impedance rise — never the Fs resonance peak (that needs an LCR notch).
  • Passive crossovers assume flat impedance; when the real impedance climbs, the crossover point shifts.
  • Use non-inductive resistors (5–10 W for home audio) and polypropylene film capacitors (100 V+).

What Is a Zobel Network?

Definition: A Zobel network — called a Boucherot cell in some texts — is a series resistor-capacitor circuit wired in parallel with a speaker driver to cancel its rising inductive impedance. Otto Zobel developed it at Bell Labs in the 1920s for line equalization; loudspeaker designers adopted it because it makes the amplifier see a nearly constant resistive load instead of an impedance that climbs with frequency.

The correction is complementary: as frequency rises, the capacitor's impedance falls at the same rate the voice coil's inductive impedance climbs. The two cancel, leaving the resistor's flat value. The network draws no current at DC and increasing current with frequency — mirroring the coil's behavior in reverse.

How This Calculator Works

This calculator sizes a Zobel from two inputs: the driver's nominal impedance and the frequency where you want flattening centered. The resistor equals the driver's DC resistance (approximated by nominal impedance); the capacitor is chosen so the R-C corner lands at your target frequency.

Methodology: Rz = Z nominal (Ω); Cz = 1 ÷ (2π × R × f) farads, displayed in microfarads. We verified this calculator's output against hand calculations using 5 sample input combinations — including the 8 Ω / 1000 Hz → 19.9 µF reference — and every result matched. Last reviewed: October 2026.

Zobel Formula and Worked Example

Rz = Re (driver DC resistance, ≈ nominal impedance)
Cz = 1 ÷ (2 × π × R × f)

Worked example: 8 Ω tweeter, flatten from 1,000 Hz:

  1. Resistor: Rz = 8 Ω
  2. Capacitor: Cz = 1 ÷ (2 × 3.1416 × 8 × 1000) = 1 ÷ 50,265 = 19.9 µF
  3. Pick standard values: 8 Ω + 20 µF — close enough; Zobels are forgiving
  4. Second check — 4 Ω midrange at 2,000 Hz: Cz = 1 ÷ (2 × π × 4 × 2000) = 19.9 µF with a 4 Ω resistor

Notice the symmetry: halving impedance while doubling frequency gives the same capacitor — the product R × f is what matters, so 8 Ω @ 1 kHz and 4 Ω @ 2 kHz need the identical 19.9 µF.

The two Zobel formulas are the same formula. Cz = 1 ÷ (2πRf) and Cz = Le ÷ R² become identical when the target frequency equals the driver's own R-L corner, f = Re ÷ (2πLe). Proof with real numbers — a driver with Re = 6 Ω and Le = 0.5 mH:

  1. Corner frequency: f = 6 ÷ (2 × 3.1416 × 0.0005) = 1,910 Hz
  2. Frequency form: Cz = 1 ÷ (2 × 3.1416 × 6 × 1910) = 13.9 µF
  3. Inductance form: Cz = 0.0005 ÷ 36 = 13.9 µF

Same answer both ways — use whichever inputs you have: nominal impedance + target frequency, or measured Re + Le.

How to Use This Calculator

  1. Enter the speaker's nominal impedance — the driver's rated ohms (4, 8, or 16).
  2. Enter the target frequency — where flattening should center, near the crossover region (1–3 kHz for tweeters).
  3. Read Rz and Cz — then round to the nearest standard component values.
  4. Wire the resistor and capacitor in series with each other, then place that pair in parallel with the driver.
  5. Position the network after the crossover, directly across the driver's terminals.

Why Speaker Impedance Rises (With Numbers)

A voice coil is an inductor as well as a resistor. Its impedance grows with frequency: Z = √(Re² + (2πfLe)²), where Le is coil inductance. The numbers get dramatic fast:

Worked example: woofer with Re = 6 Ω, Le = 0.5 mH, at 10 kHz: inductive reactance = 2 × π × 10,000 × 0.0005 = 31.4 Ω → total Z = √(36 + 986) ≈ 32.9 Ω. Your "8 Ω" driver looks like 33 Ω to the amplifier up high.

Passive crossovers are designed against a nominal impedance — when the real impedance triples, filter slopes shift, the crossover point moves, and tweeter level drops. The Zobel holds the impedance near Re so the crossover sees what the designer assumed.

What a Zobel Does NOT Fix

A Zobel tames inductive rise — and nothing else. Every driver carries a second impedance feature the Zobel cannot touch: the resonance peak at Fs, where impedance spikes for a mechanical reason (cone resonance), not an electrical one (inductance).

The resonance peak needs a series LCR notch filter (resistor + inductor + capacitor tuned to Fs), a different circuit entirely. Rule of thumb: use a Zobel for the rising top end of tweeters and midranges; use an LCR notch when a measured impedance peak at Fs is upsetting your crossover. Many DIY builders install one expecting the other — now you know the difference.

Choosing Components That Survive

A Zobel sits across the driver seeing full amplifier voltage — size the parts to the amplifier, not the driver. Treble energy is a small fraction of total power, so the resistor rating scales with amplifier class, not driver wattage:

Amplifier powerResistor ratingWhy
Up to 50 W5 W, non-inductiveHigh-frequency content draws only watts; 5 W never breaks a sweat.
50–150 W10 W, non-inductiveThe standard DIY sweet spot — covers nearly all home systems.
150 W+ / PA20 W+, non-inductiveSustained treble drive heats the resistor; mount it with airflow.
  • Resistor type: non-inductive wirewound (Ayrton-Perry) or metal-oxide. A standard inductive wirewound adds its own inductance — defeating the purpose of the network.
  • Capacitor: polypropylene film, 100 V DC minimum (250 V for pro/PA use). Film caps hold value over decades; electrolytics drift and have higher ESR — avoid them in Zobels.
  • Standard values are fine: calculated 19.9 µF → use 20 µF; calculated 7.3 Ω → use 7.5 Ω. Zobel behavior is gentle — 5% tolerance parts are inaudibly close.
  • Placement: solder the network directly across the driver's terminals, after the crossover — not at the amplifier end, where lead inductance pollutes the correction.

Limitations

  • Approximation, not measurement. The calculator uses nominal impedance; real Re and Le vary — for critical designs, measure the driver and compute Cz = Le ÷ Re².
  • Doesn't fix resonance peaks. Fs impedance spikes need an LCR notch, not a Zobel.
  • Most useful on tweeters/midranges. Woofers crossing over well below their inductive rise often don't need one.
  • Adds parts and cost. Two extra components per driver — in budget builds, verify with a measurement before adding.
  • Assumes linear behavior. At extreme excursion, Le itself varies (flux modulation) — no passive network corrects that.
